## Part I: Core Rules & Engine

<a id="chapter-3-core-mechanics"></a>
### Core Mechanics

In Corsair, the story unfolds through a conversation between the players and the Game Master (GM). The players narrate
the actions of their characters, describing what they want to do, where they want to go, and what they say. The GM, in
turn, narrates the world around the characters. The GM describes the environment, controls the non-player characters,
and explains how the world reacts to the players' actions.

Most of the time, this back-and-forth is all that's needed. If a player says, "I walk across the room and open the
door," and there's no reason they couldn't, the GM simply says, "Okay, you open the door and see..."

However, sometimes a character attempts an action where the outcome is uncertain. Can the character leap across a chasm?
Can they persuade a fugitive with a gun to stand down? Can they land a hit with their sidearm if they don't?
In these moments, when the drama and tension of the story hang in the balance, we turn to the dice to see what
happens. This is the heart of the game's mechanics, and it's where the rules of Corsair come into play.

#### Attributes

In Corsair, any significant feature of a person, creature, or object can be represented by an **Attribute**. Attributes
are a way to assign a numerical value to a characteristic, making it a concrete element of the game world. Anything and
everything can have Attributes.

While characters have core Attributes representing their physical and mental capabilities—**Strength**, **Agility**, **Finesse**, **Instinct**, **Knowledge**, and **Presence**—the concept extends to all corners of the game.

The six core Attributes define the fundamental physical and mental faculties of any living character:

* **Strength:** Raw physical power, muscular force, and bodily mass. Used for lifting heavy cargo, forcing jammed airlocks, overpowering opponents in hand-to-hand combat, and carrying heavy equipment.
* **Agility:** Whole-body speed, balance, and athletic coordination. Used for sprinting between cover, dodging incoming hazards, vaulting obstacles, and maneuvering in zero-gravity.
* **Finesse:** Manual dexterity, surgical precision, and fine motor control. Used for aiming firearms, picking mechanical locks, performing delicate surgery, and manipulating intricate instruments.
* **Knowledge:** Formal education, technical literacy, and deductive reasoning. Used for slicing computer security, analyzing sensor telemetry, operating starship consoles, and understanding alien relics.
* **Presence:** Force of personality, charisma, and commanding social authority. Used for negotiating deals, intimidating rivals, inspiring companions, and maintaining composure under intense pressure.
* **Instinct:** Intuition, acute situational alertness, and gut-level reflexes. Used for sensing impending ambushes, detecting hidden hazards, reading an adversary's unspoken intent, and reacting in the chaos of battle.

Beyond living characters, the concept of Attributes extends to all corners of the game. A weapon might have **Rate of Fire** and **Accuracy** as Attributes. A jetpack could have a **Thrust** Attribute, while a cybernetic arm might have a **Power** rating. A starship is defined by a whole range of Attributes, such as its **Thrusters**, **Hull**, and **Scanners**. These ratings are the primary factor in determining the outcome of an action. A higher Attribute value signifies greater capability, directly impacting the dice rolls you'll make during the game.

#### Attribute Pairs

When a situation calls for a die roll, the GM will determine the two most relevant Attributes to form an **Attribute
Pair**. This pair becomes the foundation of the test. The choice of Attributes is always dependent on the context of the
action being performed. It's important to note that the same Attribute can be selected twice if the action calls for a
focused application of that single quality. For example, lifting a heavy crate might call for a **Strength + Strength**
test, representing a pure application of raw power. Additionally, when a test specifies only a single Attribute, it should be understood as a test where both Attributes in the Attribute Pair are that specified Attribute.

Here are a few examples of how these might be combined into Attribute Pairs:

* **Accuracy (of the gun) + Finesse:** To fire a ranged weapon.
* **Manouverability (of the spaceship) + Instinct:** To carefully pilot a shuttle through a dense asteroid field.
* **Strength + Presence:** To intimidate a guard with a show of force.
* **Knowledge + Instinct:** To investigate a crime scene, look for clues and deduce what happened.

#### Tests

When the outcome of an action is uncertain, the GM will call for a test. There are three types of tests in Corsair:
Simple, Success, and Complex.

##### Simple Tests

A Simple Test is used when the outcome of an action is binary: you either succeed or you fail. There is no middle
ground. To make a Simple Test, you form a dice pool by taking a number of ten-sided dice (d10s) equal to the higher of
the two Attributes in the pair. In order to succeed, you need at least one of the dice to roll an 8 or higher (also
called a *Hit*). If it
does, you succeed, if it doesn't you fail.

Examples of Simple Tests include:

* Leaping across a chasm.
* Sneaking through a patrolled corridor.
* Disarming a trap.

##### Success Tests

A Success Test is used for actions that are certain to succeed, but the degree of success is variable. The question
isn't *if* you can do it, but *how well* you do it in the time available. For a Success Test, you form a dice pool using
a number of d10s equal to the *higher* of the two Attributes in the pair. The highest single die roll from the pool
becomes your "success points", which measures how effectively you performed the action.

Examples of Success Tests include:

* Applying first aid to a wounded crewmate to see how many wounds you can patch up.
* Spending a day in the workshop repairing your ship to see how much progress you make.
* Moving around the battlefield and seeing how much distance you cover.

##### Complex Tests

Complex Tests are for situations where you need to know both *if* an action is successful and *how* successful it is.
This is most common in combat, where you need to know if an attack hits and, if it does, how much damage it deals.

A Complex Test is a two-step process:

1. **The Action Roll:** First, you make a roll to see if you succeed at all. This is identical to a Simple Test: you
   roll a number of d10s equal to the **higher** of the two Attributes in the pair. If your highest die is a 7 or lower,
   your action fails.
2. **The Success Roll:** If your Action Roll is a success (at least one die shows 8 or higher), you then determine the
   *quality* of your success. You take a number of dice from the successful roll equal to the **lower** of the two
   Attributes. The highest value on these dice becomes your "success points."

Essentially, a Complex Test combines a Simple Test to see if you hit, and a Success Test to see how well you hit.

#### The Importance of Narration

It is crucial to understand that the GM determines the type of test and the Attribute Pair based on *how you describe
your character's actions*. Your narration matters. The choices you make in describing an action can change the
Attributes used, and therefore the dice you roll.

For example, let's say your character is trying to persuade an NPC:

* If you say, **"I try to reason with him, surely he will see my point in all this."** the GM will call for an **Presence + Knowledge** test, splashing in Knowledge for the reasoning part.
* If you instead say, **"I put on all the charm my character has and try to appeal to him that way."** the GM will call for a **Presence + Presence** test.
* If you narrate, **"I get all up in his face and make it very clear to him in no uncertain terms that he will do as I say"** the GM will call for an **Strength + Presence** test.
* And if you instead say, *"I hold my gun to his head and start counting"** the GM could still ask for the **Damage** or the **Load** (size) of the gun and say roll that plus your **Presence**.

#### Upgrades and Downgrades

Situational factors can influence the outcome of a test. A character might have a significant advantage, like using a high-quality tool, or a disadvantage, like working in a hazardous environment. These are represented by **Upgrades** and **Downgrades**.

* **Upgrade:** For each net **Upgrade**, you change one of the d10s in your dice pool to a twelve-sided die (d12). If all the dice in your pool are already d12s, each further **Upgrade** adds a new d10 to the pool.
* **Downgrade:** For each net **Downgrade**, you change one of the d10s in your dice pool to an eight-sided die (d8). If all the dice in your pool are already d8s, each further **Downgrade** forces you to remove a d8 from the pool.

Before any roll, the GM will assess the situation and assign **Upgrades** for advantages and **Downgrades** for disadvantages. These two cancel each other out on a 1-to-1 basis. For example, if you have 3 Upgrades and 2 Downgrades, you end up with a net of 1 Upgrade.

The net number of Upgrades or Downgrades has a maximum limit of 6. For instance, if you have 8 Upgrades and 1 Downgrade, you have a net of 7 Upgrades, but you will only apply 6 to the roll.

In the rare case that you run out of all dice to downgrade, the test is considered extremely difficult. You roll two d8s and must take the lower of the two results as your final outcome.

For the standalone rules document, see [Upgrade & Downgrade](core/Upgrade_Downgrade.md).

#### Gambit

**Gambit** is a special resource that allows players to snatch victory from the jaws of defeat. It's a pool of dice that you can use to turn a failed roll into a success.

##### Earning Gambit

You earn **Gambit** by taking risks. After you roll your dice pool for a test, if any die shows its maximum possible value (e.g., a 10 on a d10, or a 12 on a d12), you have a choice. You can either use that die as part of your test as normal, or you can "bank" it for later. If you choose to bank it, you remove the die from your current roll and add it to your personal **Gambit** pool. This die can then be spent on a future roll.

##### Spending Gambit

You can spend a **Gambit** die from your pool on any test your character makes. You can declare you are spending **Gambit** at any point during the test—before you roll, after you see the results, anytime. When you spend a **Gambit** die, you simply add it to your current dice pool and roll it. Its result is added to the other dice, potentially changing the outcome of the test. 

When a player spends a **Gambit** die, and the dice pool has already been modified by **Upgrades** or **Downgrades**, the newly added **Gambit** die retains its original die type.

##### The Golden Rule of Gambit

There is one crucial restriction on using **Gambit**: **a player can either spend or gain Gambit during a test, but not both.** This means that if you spend any **Gambit** from your pool on a test, you cannot choose to bank any dice from that test to earn new **Gambit**. Conversely, if you bank a die from a roll to earn a **Gambit**, you cannot spend any **Gambit** on that same test.

##### Putting It All Together: An Example Test

Let's see how all these elements come together in play:

* **The Scenario:** Jax, a nimble gunslinger, is pinned down behind a cargo container. A hostile security drone is advancing on his position. Jax decides to pop up and take a quick shot at the drone with his high-velocity kinetic sidearm. The GM decides this is a **Complex Test**, as they need to know if the shot hits and how much damage it deals. Jax's player describes the action as a quick, precise shot, so the GM chooses the Attribute Pair: **Finesse + Accuracy**. Jax has **Finesse 4**, his sidearm has **Accuracy 3**, and he holds two d10s in his Gambit pool.
* **1. The Action Roll (Did the shot hit?):** For the first part of a Complex Test, Jax uses the higher of the two Attributes (**Finesse 4**), assembling a dice pool of **4d10**. He rolls `[2, 4, 5, 7]`. The highest result is 7 (a miss). Jax spends a Gambit die: *"I'm spending a Gambit."* He rolls the banked d10 and gets an `[8]`. His pool is now `[2, 4, 5, 7, 8]`. With an 8, the shot hits!
* **2. The Success Roll (How much damage was dealt?):** For the second part of a Complex Test, Jax uses the lower Attribute (**Accuracy 3**), taking the 3 highest dice from his pool: `[5, 7, 8]`. The highest die is an 8 (8 points of damage). Jax decides to maximize the damage: *"I'm spending my second Gambit die."* He rolls his second banked d10 and rolls a `[9]`. His Success pool is now `[5, 7, 8, 9]`. The highest result is a 9.
* **Resolution:** Jax deals 9 points of damage to the drone. Spending his banked Gambit turned a clean miss into a devastating kinetic strike.

For the standalone rules document, see [Gambit](core/Gambit.md).

#### Contesting a Test

Often, a test isn't just against a static obstacle; it's against an active opponent who is trying to interfere. When a character is the target of another character's test (like being shot at, deceived, or tackled), they have the opportunity to contest.

In order to contest an action taken by another creature, you must be in close physical proximity to its target—typically sharing the immediate space with them. For example, you can contest an attack coming at you or an ally right beside you, and you can contest an enemy's movement crossing your space. On the other hand, you cannot contest a hostile creature reviving an ally 40 meters away by shooting at them.

To perform a **Contest**, the defending creature rolls a **Simple Test** determined by the GM (called a **Contest Roll**). Each die landing **8 or higher (a Hit)** eliminates the highest die from the roll you contested.

You **resist** a test if your Contest Roll eliminates every 8+ die from the contested roll.

You can contest **Success Tests** as well. If, after contesting, no 8+ dice remain in the contested character's pool, the Success Test generates **0 Success Points**, even if there are dice that are yet to be eliminated.

You can also contest actions that would normally not require a test (such as shushing someone before they can speak, or preventing an enemy you knocked prone from standing up). When this happens, you declare your intent to contest, and the contested creature must decide if they are willing to perform a **Simple Test** to complete their action—which you can then contest—or forego it altogether.

##### Contesting Teamwork Actions: Defender's Advantage

When an adversary is targeted by a coordinated Teamwork action, the defender gains the **Defender's Advantage**:
* **Timing:** The defender's Contest Roll occurs *after* the attacking team has finalized swapping their dice.
* **Targeted Elimination:** Each Hit (8+) on the Contest Roll allows the defender to eliminate the highest die from **whichever participating attacker's pool the defender chooses**.
* **Shutting Down Attackers:** In a Complex Test, if the defender eliminates all 8+ dice from an attacker's pool, that attacker fails their Action Roll completely and **cannot proceed to the Success Roll**.

##### Contest Example

Returning to our gunslinger Jax: suppose Jax attacks a security drone, rolling `[2, 4, 5, 7, 8]`. The drone has an **Evasion** of 3 and its maneuvering thrusters flare. The GM calls for an Evasion Contest Roll (**3d10**). The drone rolls `[3, 8, 9]`—scoring two Hits (8 and 9). These two Hits eliminate Jax's two highest dice (the 8 and the 7). Jax's remaining pool is `[2, 4, 5]`. With no die of 8 or higher remaining, the attack becomes a **Failure**; the kinetic slug ricochets harmlessly off the reinforced wall behind the drone.

For the standalone rules document, see [Contest](core/Contest.md).

#### Teamwork

Sometimes, a single character isn't enough for the job. When multiple characters cooperate on the same task at the same time—forcing open a hydraulic blast door, infiltrating past a vigilant patrol, or executing a coordinated breach—they can use **Teamwork**.

##### Joining a Teamwork Action

Any character's action can be turned into a **Teamwork Test** by additional eligible characters declaring their intent to join. A character can join a Teamwork action if:

* **Proximity:** The participants are in close physical proximity to each other and their shared objective.
* **Appropriate Contribution:** Each participant rolls the Attribute Pair appropriate to their weapon, gear, or method of action. Participants do not need to roll the same Attribute Pair! For example, two characters forcing a heavy blast door might both roll **Strength + Strength**, but in a coordinated combat assault, one character might swing a warhammer (**Strength + Finesse**, Striking) while an ally thrusts with a combat knife (**Agility + Finesse**, Piercing). Because all tests evaluate Hits on an 8 or higher, dice can be swapped freely between pools regardless of which attributes generated them.
* **Shared Target or Objective:** If the action has a target or destination, it must be identical for all participants (firing on the same enemy, treating the same wounded ally, forcing the same bulkhead).
* **Logical Feasibility:** The task must be something that multiple people can realistically cooperate on simultaneously.

##### The Dice Swap

After all participants declare their intent to cooperate, everyone rolls their respective test pools simultaneously. 

Once the dice hit the table, **any two participants can swap any amount of dice between their pools**. The strategic purpose of this exchange is mutual support: characters with surplus high-value dice (8+) pass extra results to allies who rolled low, ensuring the entire team achieves its goal or shaves away vulnerabilities.

##### Contesting Teamwork: Defender's Advantage

When an adversary is targeted by a Teamwork action, the defender gains the **Defender's Advantage**:
1. **Team Rolls:** All attacking participants roll their dice pools simultaneously.
2. **Team Swaps:** Attackers coordinate and commit their dice distribution.
3. **Defender Rolls:** The defender rolls their Contest pool *after* the attackers commit their dice swap.
4. **Targeted Elimination:** Each Hit (8+) on the Contest Roll allows the defender to eliminate the highest die from **whichever participating attacker's pool the defender chooses**. The defender can focus all Hits onto a single attacker to knock them out of the test, or split Hits across multiple foes.
5. **Knocking Attackers Out:** In a Complex Test, if the defender eliminates all 8+ dice from an attacker's pool, that attacker fails their Action Roll completely and **cannot advance to the Success Roll**. Only attackers who retain an 8+ move forward to roll damage!

##### Complex Tests & Contests in Teamwork

Teamwork can be employed across both stages of a Complex Test. Surviving attackers roll their Success pools and may **swap dice between their Success pools as well**, funneling high dice onto an operative with high-damage weapons or critical effects. 

Defenders can also use Teamwork to resist incoming actions: when multiple defenders coordinate to resist an action, they roll their contest pools simultaneously, swap dice to maximize total Hits (8+), and allocate their combined eliminations against the attackers.

For details on how Teamwork functions during structured turns and action limits, see [Teamwork Actions](#teamwork-actions) under Scenes, Pacing, and Action. For the standalone rules document, see [Teamwork](core/Teamwork.md).

### Scenes, Pacing, and Action

Corsair's gameplay is structured to keep the action moving and give every character a chance to shine. The story unfolds
in **Scenes**, which are the fundamental building blocks of your adventure. However, not all scenes are paced the same.
The GM can control the narrative flow by "zooming in" or "zooming out" on the action, changing the scope of a scene from
a single, heart-pounding moment to the events of an entire day.

The game distinguishes between three types of scenes based on their scope: **Moment to Moment**, **Place to Place**, and
**Day to Day**.

#### Scene Types and Pacing

Think of the scene type as a camera lens. The GM can zoom in for a close-up on a dramatic confrontation or zoom out to
quickly cover a long journey.

* **Day to Day:** This is the most "zoomed-out" level of play. It's used to cover long periods of time in which the
  characters are engaged in ongoing activities, but nothing requires immediate, second-by-second attention. This could
  be a day spent repairing the ship, a week of interstellar travel, or a month of downtime in a bustling city.

* **Place to Place:** This is the mid-level scope, focusing on a specific location and the events that unfold there.
  It's used for exploring a space station, navigating a dangerous asteroid field, or investigating a crime scene. Time
  is more fluid here than in Moment to Moment play, but the focus is on a single, contiguous area.

* **Moment to Moment:** This is the most "zoomed-in" level of play, where every second counts. This is for high-stakes
  situations like combat, a frantic chase, or a tense negotiation.

#### Action Points

Action Points are the currency you spend to perform significant actions, especially those requiring a Test. Every player
has a maximum of 3 Action Points.

Every test you take, whether it be Simple, Success, or Complex, requires 1 Action Point.

In some cases the GM might also call on you to spend an Action Point without taking a test, for example, to
transit from one major location to the next.

#### Rounds

Within any scene, the action is broken down into **Rounds**. A round is a period of in-game time appropriate to the
scene's pacing, where every character present has an opportunity to act. This ensures that the chaos of a situation is
manageable and that the spotlight is shared.

A round in moment-to-moment pacing is only a dozen or so seconds long. In place-to-place pacing, a round might last from
minutes to a few hours, while in day-to-day pacing, it's a full day.

At the start of every Round, every creature refills their Action Points to the maximum.

When the round ends, the GM gets an opportunity to introduce new characters into the scene, based on the broader scope
of the scene that's playing out and the amount of time that passed during the round. This can be new reinforcements
arriving in a moment-to-moment gunfight, or an inspection from the local authorities while you are going place-to-place.
This also lets the GM advance the plot via NPCs that are acting outside the ongoing scene.

#### Turns

Each round is divided into multiple turns of two different types: The **Player Turn** and the **GM Turn**.

* **Player Turn:** During the Player Turn, the players decide and declare what their characters are doing. A crucial
  aspect of the Player Turn is its free-form nature. **Players can act in any order they wish.** You can coordinate your
  actions, set up combos, and react to each other's decisions dynamically. One player might act, then another, then the
  first player might act again.

* **GM Turn:** During the GM Turn, the Game Master narrates the actions of the non-player characters (NPCs) in the
  scene. This is when every NPC makes their moves. If there are no NPCs in the scene other than the players, the GM
  turn is skipped, and it's the Player's Turn again.

The turn swaps from the players to the GM (or vice versa) after the active side has spent a total of 2 or more Action
Points and resolved their last action. The round concludes when both sides choose to "pass," meaning they have no
further actions to take. Every round starts with the Player Turn first.

Most rounds will fly by due to the players being grounded with no NPCs, or NPCs that generally don't wish to affect
the player characters. Therefore, most rounds will be rather one-sided, but that is a good thing as the GM already has
a lot of work just arbitrating the player characters' narrations.

When a player action causes an NPC to go from a passive to an active participant in the scene, they become active
and receive their Action Points next round, meaning each player character has a chance to act before the GM gets to
react with NPCs.

#### Going First

During the first turn of every round, both the players and the GM can state they would like to go first.
If they do, they have to bid an Action Point, from any creature in the scene that they control. If only one side bids,
they automatically go first, but the Action Point is considered to be spent, counting against the 2 AP per Turn limit.

If both sides bid, the creatures bidding roll an (Agility + Instinct) Simple Test. The side with more dice landing 8 or
higher goes first. For the loosing side, this counts as an AP spent out of turn, meaning that their AP is gone, but it
doesn't count against their 2 AP limit for the next turn.

In case of a tie, or when neither side bids, the players go first.

#### Teamwork Actions

Teamwork allows multiple characters to contribute to a single goal. When characters use Teamwork, they can spend their
Action Points to contribute to the collective effort. While players spending Action Points to participate in a Teamwork
Test counts towards the 2AP per Turn limit, reaching the limit doesn't stop additional characters from participating.
The turn will only end once the Teamwork Test has resolved.

**Example:** The crew needs to get through a crowded checkpoint without drawing attention. The GM rules this will be a
Teamwork action, requiring each character to make a Success Test to see how well they blend in.

* Silas, a master of disguise, decides to create a distraction and scores 2 hits.
* Griz, a large and imposing figure, tries to look inconspicuous by pretending to be a simple cargo hauler, but
  fails miserably, socring no hits.
* Jax, ever the opportunist, tries to flirt with one of the guards to divert their attention, scoring just one hit.

The players have now collectively spent 3 Action Points. Silas swaps one of their hits for one of Griz's dice,
representing them covering for each other's mistakes and successfully navigating the checkpoint as a cohesive unit.

The flexibility of the turn structure and Action Point system is designed to encourage creative problem-solving and
dynamic, cinematic action. Don't be afraid to combine actions, work together, and push your characters to their limits.
For full rules on coordinating actions, swapping dice, and how defenders contest Teamwork actions using Defender's Advantage, see [Teamwork](core/Teamwork.md).

#### Delayed Actions

During high-stakes encounters, characters do not always act immediately; often, a disciplined Corsair holds their fire until an enemy leaves cover, waits for an incoming threat to breach a threshold, or readies a shot to interrupt a hostile move. You can prepare for such moments by declaring a **Delayed Action**.

* **Setting Up (1 AP):** On your turn, you spend **1 Action Point** to prepare a delayed action. When declaring a delayed action, you must specify two things:
  1. **The Trigger:** The observable event or condition you are reacting to (e.g., *"when an enemy steps out of cover,"* *"if Brant draws a sidearm,"* or *"when the blast door starts to open"*).
  2. **The Planned Action:** The specific action you intend to take when the trigger occurs. The planned action must be manageable within **1 AP** (such as taking a shot with a firearm, swinging a melee weapon, making a Simple Test, or moving up to your base movement distance).

* **Declaring Timing (Before vs. After):** You have tactical freedom over when your action resolves relative to the trigger, but you must declare your timing beforehand when setting it up:
  * **Reacting Before (Interrupt):** You act *before* the triggering event resolves. For instance, declaring that you will shoot at a target the moment they attempt to fire at you allows you to shoot first, potentially eliminating or disrupting them before their attack can land.
  * **Reacting After (Response):** You act *after* the triggering event has completed. For instance, waiting until an enemy has moved completely out from behind heavy cover into open ground before firing ensures they cannot claim cover bonuses against your attack.

* **Action Point & Turn Accounting:** The 1 AP cost is paid **on the turn you set up the delayed action**, counting against the 2 AP limit of that turn. When the trigger condition occurs and you take your action, it requires **0 additional AP** in that moment, and it does not count against the AP limit of the turn in which it triggers.

* **Resolution and Expiration:** When the trigger condition is met, you immediately execute the declared test or action. If the triggering condition does not occur before the end of the round, the prepared action expires without effect—the spent Action Point represents the time, readiness, and vigilance invested in holding your position.

* **Delayed Teamwork Actions:** Multiple characters can coordinate to set up a shared delayed action (such as establishing a crossfire overwatch or setting a synchronized ambush).
  * **Setup:** All participants must share the same space, spend **1 AP** each, and agree upon the exact same **Trigger**, **Target**, and **Timing** (Before or After). Each participant prepares the action and Attribute Pair appropriate to their weapon or tool. An ally can spend 1 AP on a subsequent turn to join an existing ally's delayed action if they enter their space.
  * **Triggering & Swapping:** When the trigger condition occurs, all linked participants roll simultaneously for **0 additional AP** and can swap dice between their pools under standard Teamwork rules. If the target contests, Defender's Advantage applies normally.
  * **Disruption:** If an individual participant is incapacitated, knocked out of the space, or forced to defend before the trigger occurs, their readied action is broken, but remaining allies can still execute their delayed action when the condition is met.

#### Zooming In and Out

The GM can transition between scene types as the story demands.

* **Zooming In:** When a broader scene suddenly becomes more dangerous or dramatic, the GM can "zoom in." For example, while the crew is exploring a derelict ship (Place to Place), they might be ambushed by security drones, instantly shifting the scene to Moment to Moment. When zooming in, all characters present in the scene synchronize their broader Action Points down to the lowest remaining AP among them (reflecting time elapsed together). They then refill their Action Points in the new, narrower pacing to their maximum (3 AP) and begin a fresh round. Any ongoing effects created in the broader pacing are retained.

* **Zooming Out:** When a high-stakes scene concludes, the GM "zooms out" back to the broader pacing. Players set aside their tactical Action Points and resume the broader pacing with their remaining broader Action Points. If characters still have Action Points remaining in that broader round, they continue their shift or day; if all broader AP have been spent, the broader round concludes at the End of the Round. Characters operating in parallel in other locations retain their unspent broader AP and can take actions during that same timeframe.

### Effects

*You can download and print the [Effects Quick Reference Sheet](core/Effects_Sheet.html) for play.*

While succeeding at a test achieves your immediate objective, game mechanics in Corsair invite you to shape the battleground, create tactical openings, and alter the narrative flow of a scene. This is accomplished through **Effects**. Rather than relying solely on standard outcomes, Effects allow you to convert exceptional rolls into dynamic opportunities—turning a single strike into a sweeping attack, driving an opponent backward, or setting up a defensive barrier for an ally.

#### Activating Effects

To activate an Effect, you sacrifice **Hits**—dice displaying a result of 8 or higher—rolled during an Action Roll or a Success Roll, depending on the specific Effect's requirements.

Activating an Effect is an exercise in collaborative storytelling between you and the Game Master. The Effect you declare must align logically with your character's action, declared narration, and chosen Attribute Pair. For instance, you cannot use an attack roll with a rifle to push a heavy blast door shut across the room, but you could easily describe forcing an enemy backward into cover or shooting out a control panel. The GM retains final discretion on whether an Effect fits naturally into the unfolding fiction.

#### Action Effects

Action Effects are activated during your **Action Roll**. By setting aside Hits before moving on to resolve your Success Roll, you can manipulate the immediate environment or extend the reach of your action:

* **Chain Effect:** When performing an action directed at a single target out of several eligible choices—most commonly during combat—you may spend Hits on a **Chain Effect**. For each Hit you sacrifice from your Action Roll, you select one additional eligible target to include in the action. When chaining a Complex Test, perform a single Success Roll using the lower Attribute; the highest die applies individually to every target caught in the chain. You cannot chain actions that naturally affect multiple targets by default. Furthermore, if the base action requires expending a specific resource (such as ammunition, power cells, or consumable gear), you must pay that resource cost separately for each chained target. When making a ranged attack, you can only chain targets in the same range band, and the combined use of Chain and Crit effects cannot exceed your weapon's Rate of Fire (RoF).
* **Upgrade and Downgrade Effect:** When your action creates a clear situational advantage for yourself or an impending disadvantage for an enemy, you can activate an **Upgrade or Downgrade Effect**. Each Hit spent from your Action Roll allows you to step up or step down a die on subsequent tests related to that altered circumstance. Crucially, if you are resolving a Complex Test, you may apply this effect immediately to step up dice on your own upcoming Success Roll without needing to specify the situational advantage.
* **Blocker Effect:** If your narration aims to hinder, interrupt, or prevent an enemy from carrying out a specific future action, you can establish a **Blocker Effect**. For every **2 Hits** you sacrifice from your Action Roll, you set aside 1 Hit to create a pool of defensive dice. When an opponent attempts the blocked action, each set-aside Hit negates the single highest die from their test pool before resolving. Once a set-aside Hit negates an opponent's die, that set-aside Hit is expended. The Blocker Effect persists until all set-aside Hits are exhausted. If an opponent attempts to perform an action that normally does not require a test (such as opening a latch or standing up), the Blocker forces them to execute a **Simple Test** first; failing to eliminate all set-aside Hits prevents them from completing the action.

#### Sustained Effects

Certain Effects can be sustained over time rather than depleting after a single use. An Effect can become a **Sustained Effect** whenever there is an active physical object (such as magnetic restraints, deployed gear, a smoke screen, or specialized equipment) or an ongoing continuous action sustaining it.

When an Effect is sustained, any set-aside Hits or mechanical benefits do not deplete when triggered. Instead, the full pool of set-aside Hits or advantages resets after each affected action or attempt. Sustained Effects can be applied to any Effect type where continuous operation makes logical narrative sense—most commonly Blocker Effects or ongoing Upgrade/Downgrade Effects. A Sustained Effect remains active until an opponent succeeds despite the penalty or until the physical object or continuous action sustaining it is removed, destroyed, or neutralized.

#### Success Effects

Success Effects are activated during your **Success Roll**, allowing you to amplify the effectiveness or intensity of an action that has already proven successful:

* **Crit Effect:** The primary Success Effect across all Success Rolls. For every Hit (8+) sacrificed from your Success Roll pool, you add **+2 Success Points** directly to your highest remaining die value.
* **Push Effect:** Leverage raw momentum or physical force to reposition a target. For each Hit sacrificed from your Success Roll, you knock or force the target backward by up to 4 meters. If you wish to maintain pressure or stay in close proximity, you may move along with the target as they are displaced without spending additional movement. Push Effects can only be activated if **Strength** (or a similar physical power attribute) is included in the test's Attribute Pair; they cannot be activated with tests relying solely on finesse, mental, or social attributes.

#### Removing Effects

Ongoing environmental hazards, physical restraints, and persistent blockers do not have to remain forever. If a character is in a position to eliminate or neutralize an active ongoing effect, they may spend **1 Action Point** on their turn to make a **Simple Test** determined by the GM. Each Hit scored on this Simple Test removes 1 set-aside Hit from the targeted effect, gradually breaking down the obstacle until it is completely eliminated.

#### Combined Mechanics in Action

> Jax and his team are attempting to breach a secure corporate research vault defended by automated security drones. Engaging in close combat at the armored bulkhead, Jax declares, *"I step in and swing my axe in a sweeping melee strike across the lead drone to tear open its plating while driving it back into its squadmate to clear the entryway."* 
> 
> The GM assesses the melee action and calls for a **Strength (5) + Strength (5)** Complex Test. Jax rolls 5d10 for his Action Roll based on his higher Attribute (**Strength 5**), producing `[3, 5, 8, 8, 9]`. 
> 
> With three Hits (`8`, `8`, and `9`), Jax spends one Hit (`8`) to activate an Action Effect—an **Upgrade Effect**—stepping up one die on his upcoming Success Roll from a d10 to a **d12**. He leaves his remaining Hits (`8` and `9`) to ensure his Action Roll succeeds.
> 
> Moving to his Success Roll for his lower Attribute (**Strength 5**), Jax rolls 4d10 and 1d12 (modified by his Upgrade Effect). His roll yields `[5, 6, 7, 8]` on the d10s and `8` on the d12. 
> 
> Jax scores two Hits (both `8`s) on his Success Roll. He decides to activate two Success Effects:
> 1. He spends his first `8` to activate a **Push Effect** (valid since his test relies on Strength), shoving the lead drone back 4 meters directly into its squadmate.
> 2. He spends his second `8` to activate a **Crit Effect**, granting **+2 Success Points** to his highest remaining die.
> 
> The highest remaining die on his Success Roll is `7`. By sacrificing his second `8` for Crit instead of keeping it as his base die, Jax increases his total from 8 to **9 Success Points** (7 + 2 = 9), dealing heavy damage to the drone while driving it clear of the doorway.

## Part II: Character Creation & Classes

### Player Characters

Your character is your avatar in the world of Corsair—the protagonist of the stories you forge across the pressurized corridors, lawless frontier moons, and ancient orbital ruins of the Sphere. Their attributes, combat chassis, skills, and hard-earned traits determine whether they survive their next contract or end up as unrecovered scrap drifting in the black.

#### Character Creation & Modes of Play

Before rolling dice or allocating statistics, the gaming group selects their campaign's **Mode of Play**, defining the crew's legal standing, resources, and operational freedom:

* **Freelancers (Aspirants):** Independent scrappers, frontier bounty hunters, or unregistered guns-for-hire operating on the margins. Freelancers possess no corporate patrons, no legal immunity, and no initial starship, clawing their way up through local surface contracts and freelance odd jobs.
* **Corsairs (Chartered Cell):** Fully licensed operatives holding an official Corsair Charter issued by a Sovereign Colony, Megacorporation, or the Flotilla. Chartered Corsairs start with assigned mission briefs, institutional backing, and a shared starship—balanced by demanding corporate oversight, quota audits, and political entanglements.
* **Rogue Corsairs (Outlaws):** Disavowed operatives whose charters were burned, revoked, or violently betrayed. Running with a hot starship and forged transponder codes, Rogue Corsairs navigate underworld syndicates while staying one step ahead of corporate kill-teams.

Building an operative follows eight structured steps:
1. **Choose Mode of Play & Sponsor:** Establish your crew's legal standing and patron (e.g., Flotilla Admiralty, Suncatcher Cartels, Nimbus Aerostats, or Freelance).
2. **Select Lineage:** Choose your species background—such as adaptive Humans, stoic and cybernetically dense Orks, four-armed scavengers (Scav), radiotrophic X'ara Ancestrals, or synthetic Automata.
3. **Select a MANTLE Chassis:** Choose your character's class rig (Conduit, Hazard, Pilot, Vanguard, or Weaver).
4. **Distribute Core Attributes:** Allocate points among the six core attributes on a scale from 1 to 10.
5. **Calculate Derived Attributes:** Determine **Endurance** (second-highest Physical Attribute) and **Willpower** (second-highest Mental Attribute).
6. **Assign Skills:** Link your trained skill proficiencies to specific Attribute Pairs, creating narrative specializations rated 1 to 3.
7. **Equip Starter Gear:** Select a starter kit and manage equipment within your maximum carrying **Load**.
8. **Invest Starting XP:** Spend starting Experience Points to purchase flat-rate operational **Traits**.

For the complete step-by-step creation walkthrough, see **[Character Creation](character/Creation.md)**. You can track your operative using the printable **[Character Sheet](character/Character_Sheet.html)** and maintain your crew's vessel and stations on the **[Crew Sheet](character/Crew_Sheet.html)**.

#### Classes & The MANTLE System

In *Corsair*, an operative's class is not an abstract magical tradition; it is defined by a proprietary **MANTLE** (*Modular Adaptive Neural Transmission Linked Equipment*). A MANTLE is an advanced technological and cybernetic chassis hardwired directly into the operative's spine and central nervous system through a permanent **Neural Control Chip (NCC)**. The five specialized MANTLE chassis provide distinct tactical omniroles:

* **[Conduit](classes/Conduit.md)** ([Class Sheet](classes/Conduit_Sheet.html)): Living bio-electrical capacitors and shock troopers. Conduits channel high-voltage currents, generate localized electromagnetic interference pulses, overload hostile electronic systems, and ground dangerous energy discharges through superconducting carbon chassis.
* **[Hazard](classes/Hazard.md)** ([Class Sheet](classes/Hazard_Sheet.html)): Combat alchemists and chemical warfare specialists. Hazard operatives wield pressurized pneumatic projectors to disperse corrosive neurotoxins, incendiary aerosols, fast-acting coagulant foams, and tailored combat stimulants across the battlefield.
* **[Pilot](classes/Pilot.md)** ([Class Sheet](classes/Pilot_Sheet.html)): Neuro-synced exoskeleton and drone riggers. Pilots interface their consciousness directly with autonomous combat drones, deploy perimeter automated sentry turrets, and hard-jack into starship helm matrices with split-second neural response times.
* **[Vanguard](classes/Vanguard.md)** ([Class Sheet](classes/Vanguard_Sheet.html)): Frontline assault breakers and kinetic point-men. Vanguards deploy morphing kinetic tower shields, anchor portable barricades, execute concussive battering-ram breaches, and draw hostile fire away from vulnerable squaddie positions.
* **[Weaver](classes/Weaver.md)** ([Class Sheet](classes/Weaver_Sheet.html)): Molecular fabrication artisans and nanite swarm controllers. Weavers command clouds of programmable micro-machines to spin carbon mono-filament tripwires, weld ruptured bulkheads shut, dissolve enemy armor plates, and repair damaged field gear mid-firefight.

For detailed origins, neuro-biological side effects, and ability catalogs, see **[Classes & The MANTLE System](classes/Classes.md)**.

#### Traits

Traits represent specialized combat reflexes, physiological quirks, underworld connections, and tactical habits that give your character distinct narrative permissions and mechanical advantages. 

Unlike traditional tiered skill trees, *Corsair* utilizes a flat Experience Point (XP) pricing model: each Trait has a fixed XP cost. Characters can purchase Traits during character creation or acquire them between missions through downtime study and physical conditioning. 

See the complete catalog of operational capabilities in **[Traits](character/Traits.md)** ([Printable Traits Sheet](character/Traits_Sheet.html)).

#### Character Advancement

Survival in the Sphere is rewarded through experience. Operatives gain Experience Points (XP) by completing contract milestones, retrieving intact Precursor data cores, surviving catastrophic shipboard emergencies, and investing downtime shifts into dedicated training. 

XP is spent directly to increment Core Attributes, increase Skill ratings, or unlock new Traits. Because progression costs scale gradually, operatives naturally broaden their competencies or hone razor-sharp tactical specializations.

For full advancement tables and training requirements, see **[Character Advancement](character/Advancement.md)**.

#### Core Attributes

Every entity in Corsair is defined by six Core Attributes rated from 1 to 10 (where 1 represents the bare minimum functional baseline and 10 represents the pinnacle of mortal physiological capability):

##### Physical Attributes
* **Strength:** Raw muscular power, bone density, and mechanical torque. Used to force jammed airlocks, grapple armored adversaries, steady heavy support weapons, and calculate maximum carrying Load.
* **Agility:** Whole-body speed, balance, and kinetic reflexes. Governs sprint speed, evasive tumbling, vaulting across low-gravity debris, and zero-g maneuvering.
* **Finesse:** Manual precision, fine motor coordination, and delicate touch. Crucial for aiming ranged firearms, slipping blades through armor seams, executing surgical first aid, and picking mechanical locks.

##### Mental Attributes
* **Knowledge:** Formal technical education, scientific literacy, and rapid data synthesis. Used to hack encrypted corporate nodes, analyze alien ruins, repair complex reactor turbines, and identify biological toxins.
* **Presence:** Force of personality, command authority, and psychological gravitas. Essential for rallying panicked allies, intimidating armed extortionists, negotiating contract bounties, and deceiving station customs officials.
* **Instinct:** Intuition, spatial awareness, and raw sensory alertness. Used to detect cloaked sensor drones, anticipate impending ambushes, read micro-expressions during tense interrogations, and make split-second pilot corrections.

#### Derived Attributes

From your six Core Attributes, you derive two vital defensive statistics:

* **Endurance:** Your physical resilience and shock tolerance. Equal to your **second-highest Physical Attribute** (Strength, Agility, or Finesse).
* **Willpower:** Your mental discipline, composure under fire, and psychological fortitude. Equal to your **second-highest Mental Attribute** (Knowledge, Presence, or Instinct).

#### Skills

While attributes measure natural talent, Skills represent disciplined training and combat muscle memory. Skills are rated from 1 to 3:

* **Skill 1 (Trained):** Competent field experience; grants **1 automatic Upgrade** on tests using the linked Attribute Pair.
* **Skill 2 (Professional):** Seasoned veteran mastery; grants **2 automatic Upgrades** on tests using the linked Attribute Pair.
* **Skill 3 (Master):** Elite system-wide renown; grants **3 automatic Upgrades** on tests using the linked Attribute Pair.

##### Flexible Narrative Naming
Players invent creative, thematic names for their skills that logically connect to their chosen Attribute Pair (e.g., *Kinetic Marksmanship* for **Finesse + Accuracy**, *Breach Breaker* for **Strength + Finesse**, or *Corporate Espionage* for **Knowledge + Presence**). While the name cements character identity and roleplay flavor, its mechanical bonus applies universally to any test leveraging that linked Attribute Pair.

#### Condition & Harm

*Corsair* eschews abstract, bloated hit-point pools in favor of a tactile, high-consequence **Condition** engine. Resilience is tracked across two parallel categories: **Physical Condition** (injuries, blood loss, and kinetic trauma governed by **Endurance**) and **Mental Condition** (panic, neurological fatigue, and psychological shock governed by **Willpower**).

##### The Three-Track Model
Both Physical and Mental Condition are divided into three linked, sequential **Tracks** (Track I, Track II, and Track III). Whenever a character suffers damage or stress, segments are marked off starting on Track I. Each track has a fixed capacity equal to:

$$\text{Track Capacity} = 4 + \text{Derived Attribute}$$

*(e.g., an operative with Endurance 3 has three Physical Tracks of 7 segments each).*

##### Light Wounds vs. Aggravated Wounds
Harm is categorized into two distinct severities:
* **Light Wounds / Stress:** Standard operational wear, bruises, minor lacerations, and momentary shock.
* **Aggravated Wounds / Stress:** Deep bone fractures, ruptured internal organs, severe neurotoxin damage, and traumatic psychological breaks. When an incoming hit overflows the remaining capacity of a Track, the excess overflow converts existing Light Wounds on that filled track into **Aggravated Wounds**! Aggravated harm cannot be cleared with basic field stims or short rests.

##### Attribute Penalties: Impaired & Crippled
Pushing a track to its limit compromises the operative's effectiveness:
* **Impaired (Light Wounds):** When an individual Track fills completely with Light Wounds, the player marks one linked Attribute (Physical for Physical Tracks, Mental for Mental Tracks) as **Impaired**. Any subsequent test using an Impaired Attribute suffers **1 Downgrade**.
* **Crippled (Aggravated Wounds):** When an individual Track fills completely with Aggravated Wounds, that Attribute becomes **Crippled**, imposing **2 Downgrades** on all associated rolls.

##### Recovery Scopes
* **Moment-to-Moment:** Rapid combat stim-packs and specialized class abilities restore immediate segments during firefights.
* **Place-to-Place (Short Rest):** A breather during exploration allows a **Success Test** using **Endurance + Willpower**; every 2 Success Points heals 1 Light Wound or Light Stress segment.
* **Day-to-Day (Long Rest):** Extended downtime rest allows an **Endurance + Willpower** Success Test. Generated Success Points can be allocated freely: **1 SP per Light segment**, or **2 SP per Aggravated segment**. Medbays and station trauma clinics provide substantial additional recovery bonuses.

##### Total Track Collapse
* If all three **Physical Tracks** fill completely with damage, the operative falls **Unconscious**. If all three Physical Tracks fill completely with **Aggravated Wounds**, the operative **Dies**.
* If all three **Mental Tracks** fill completely, the operative is overwhelmed and **cannot earn or spend Gambit Dice**. If all three Mental Tracks fill completely with **Aggravated Stress**, mental resilience shatters, leaving the operative fully **susceptible to hostile commands, intimidation, and interrogation**.

For full condition rules and recovery procedures, see **[Condition](character/Condition.md)**.

## Part III: Advanced Operations: Combat, Hardware & Downtime

### Combat

In the world of Corsair, conflict is inevitable. Whether repelling a boarding party in a zero-gravity cargo bay, breaching a corporate research vault, or trading fire through the neon-lit alleys of Crossroads, violent clashes demand precision and tactical positioning. When a conflict turns violent and every second counts, play shifts into **Moment to Moment** pacing. While combat can be resolved via "theatre of the mind," tactical encounters are optimized for a 2-meter grid battle map.

#### Movement & Verticality

In combat, an operative can spend **1 Action Point (AP)** to move. The character executes a **Success Test**—typically using **Agility**—and advances a total distance equal to their base speed (**4 meters / 1 space**) plus additional spaces determined by the test result (divide rolled Success Points by 4, rounded up). A single movement action can achieve up to 16 meters (4 spaces).
* **Vertical Movement:** Multi-level environments (gantry catwalks, elevator shafts, ship compartments) are structured in vertical 2-meter layers. Ascending or climbing between layers is treated as Difficult Terrain. Falling more than 2 meters inflicts damage equal to **three times the fall distance in meters**, which bypasses armor Protection entirely.
* **Flying:** Thruster harnesses and jump-packs allow flight across 3D space, ignoring difficult terrain. Ascending costs double movement (4 meters of movement per 2 meters of climb). Losing flight mid-turn allows an operative to maintain position until the end of the turn, after which they fall.
* **Contesting Movement:** When a creature attempts to move through an enemy's space, the adversary can spend 1 AP to contest the movement (e.g., using **Strength** to grapple or **Agility** to trip). Each Hit (8+) on the contest roll eliminates the highest die from the mover's Success Test; if no 8+ dice remain, the mover is stopped dead in their tracks in the enemy's space.
* **Difficult Terrain:** Dense rubble, magnetic debris, waist-deep slurry, and waist-high barricades cost **double movement** (8 meters of movement per 4-meter space).

#### Space, Occupancy & Outnumbering

Tactical positioning on the battle map is governed by physical space constraints:
* **Capacity:** A standard 4-meter diameter space accommodates up to **4 standard-sized humanoids** (Large creatures occupy the space of 2; Huge creatures occupy the space of 4).
* **Outnumbering Advantage:** Surrounding an enemy yields immense tactical leverage. When allied combatants outnumber hostiles within a single space, every ally receives a number of **Upgrades** on melee attack tests equal to the numeric difference between allies and enemies in that space.
* **Melee Attacks:** Melee combat occurs within the same space or against an adjacent space. Striking a foe in an adjacent space pulls the attacker into that space (if capacity allows). All melee attacks pair two distinct Physical Attributes, unlocking bonus damage when matched to the weapon's favored form: **Striking** (**Strength + Finesse**), **Slashing** (**Agility + Strength**), and **Piercing** (**Agility + Finesse**).

#### Close Quarters Combat (CQC) & Cover

* **Close Quarters Combat (CQC):** Discharging a firearm while sharing a space with a hostile adversary imposes **2 Downgrades** on the attack roll. Firing from outside into a chaotic space where allies and enemies are engaged in melee imposes **1 Downgrade**.
* **Cover:** Solid environmental features grant flat damage reduction (**Protection**) that stacks with worn armor against attacks originating outside the space:
  * **Light Cover:** +1 Protection (metal pipes, thin crates, light barricades).
  * **Medium Cover:** +2 Protection (reinforced bulkheads, heavy engine blocks).
  * **Heavy Cover:** +3 Protection (armored blast bunkers, reinforced ferro-concrete pillars).
* **Cover Degradation & Facing:** Cover is directional; the initial incoming attack establishes the "covering side." Attacks crossing adjacent sides grant reduced Protection (-1 on hex grids, -2 on square grids), while attacks from opposite sides bypass cover completely. Cover can be destroyed: Light, Medium, and Heavy Cover possess 12, 24, and 36 HP with innate Protection of 4, 8, and 12 when directly targeted; every 12 damage inflicted reduces its Protection benefit by 1. Breaching charges and anti-materiel ordnance ignore innate cover protection.

For complete combat rules, engagement actions, and tactical examples, see **[Combat](combat/Combat.md)**.

### Equipment

Every item in *Corsair* is anchored to hard science and functional engineering. Mechanically significant hardware is governed by the **Load** system, preventing operatives from hauling walking arsenals without physiological consequence.

#### Load & Carrying Capacity

An operative's carrying capacity is strictly determined by physical conditioning:

$$\text{Maximum Load Capacity} = 12 + (\text{Strength} \times 2)$$

Carrying gear up to this limit incurs no encumbrance penalties, but exceeding this value is physically impossible. Leaving unallocated Load capacity is vital for hauling salvage, spare munitions, and high-value mission objectives back to the ship.

#### Equipment Catalogs

* **[Firearms](equipment/Firearms.md)** ([Firearms Sheet](equipment/Firearms_Sheet.html)): Kinetic projectile weapons, rail carbines, coil shotguns, and heavy squad support machine guns. Governed by **Accuracy arrays** across four range bands (Short 0–5 spaces, Medium 6–12 spaces, Long 13–20 spaces, Extreme 20+ spaces), **Rate of Fire (RoF)** which caps the combined number of Crit and Chain Effects on an attack, flat **Damage Bonuses**, and **Minimum Strength** ratings.
* **[Melee Weapons](equipment/Melee.md)** ([Melee Sheet](equipment/Melee_Sheet.html)): Vibro-blades, hydraulic shock truncheons, monomolecular daggers, and heavy boarding axes. Melee weapons deal Piercing, Slashing, or Striking damage, granting a damage bonus equal to the lower attribute of the test when rolled with their favored physical Attribute Pair.
* **[Armor](equipment/Armor.md)** ([Armor Sheet](equipment/Armor_Sheet.html)): Ballistic weaves, powered flak jackets, and mechanized hazard exoskeletons providing flat **Protection** that subtracts directly from incoming damage. Heavy plate enforces the **Cumbersome (X)** property, imposing Downgrades on Agility tests (including movement and sprint tests).
* **[Gear](equipment/Gear.md)** ([Gear Sheet](equipment/Gear_Sheet.html)): Tactical kits across seven operational domains—Mobility, Fortification, Explosives & Launchers, Medical, Infiltration & Hacking, Detection & Sensors, and Survival. Gear items provide dedicated **Item Attributes** (such as *Thrust*, *Medicine*, *Sensors*, or *Hacking*) that pair with character attributes for 1 AP tests.
* **[Exotic Hardware](equipment/Exotic_Hardware.md):** High-tier black-budget skunkworks prototypes, reverse-engineered alien alloys, and salvaged Precursor dark-matter transducing relics. Segregated into a 4-tier rarity ladder (Tier 1 Mil-Spec at 15,000 Credits up to Tier 4 Unique Wonders). Constrained by massive Load requirements and thermal cooldowns rather than arbitrary attunement caps; exotic items cannot accept aftermarket upgrade kits.
* **Maintenance & Field Repairs:** Damaged hardware, turrets, and fortifications track structure via Health Points (HP). Operatives carrying toolkits with the **Tinkering** attribute can spend 1 AP or downtime shifts to make a **Tinkering + Knowledge** Success Test, restoring **1 HP per Success Point**.

For the foundational hardware catalog, see **[Equipment](equipment/Equipment.md)**.

### Starships and Space Combat

In the vast void of the Sphere, your starship is your mobile base, your orbital dropship, and your crew's only shield against the lethal vacuum of space. Space combat in *Corsair* is grounded in Newtonian orbital mechanics, thrust vectors, and physiological g-force thresholds.

#### Macro-Grid & Vector Momentum

Space battles begin on the **Macro-Grid**, where each hex represents a 1-kilometer diameter sphere:
* **Acceleration & Movement:** Ships move across the grid using their engine's **Acceleration** attribute via a **Success Test**. A vessel moves 500 meters per Success Point (or 1 hex per 2 Success Points, rounded down).
* **G-Strain:** High-acceleration maneuvers punish human and alien biology alike. If an acceleration maneuver expends more than **6 Success Points**, the crew suffers severe g-force strain: every additional Success Point spent inflicts **1 Light Wound and 1 Light Stress** on every crew member, bypassing armor Protection entirely.
* **Newtonian Forward Commitment:** Due to vector inertia, a vessel cannot immediately re-enter a hex it vacated during the same movement action; it must traverse at least two adjacent hexes before looping back.

#### The Dogfight Scale

When two or more hostile ships enter the same 1 km macro-hex, combat transitions immediately into the micro-scale **Dogfight Mode**, tracked on an abstract relative scale from 1 to 10:

$$\text{[1 – 2 – 3 Disadvantage (Target on Tail)]} \longrightarrow \text{[4 – 5 – 6 – 7 Neutral Vectors]} \longrightarrow \text{[8 – 9 – 10 Advantage (On Target's Tail)]}$$

* **Shifting Position:** Pilots execute Complex Tests using **Maneuverability + Instinct** to climb or drop positions on the scale (1 position per Success Point). G-Strain applies if exceeding 6 SP.
* **Interception Advantage:** A hunter moving into an occupied hex starts at **Position 6** (Attacker), while the defending vessel starts at **Position 5**.
* **Breakaway:** A ship trapped at a disadvantage cannot simply burn away into the void. A pilot must climb back to neutral airspace (**Position 4 or higher**) before they can spend remaining acceleration points to break away back onto the Macro-Grid.

#### Starship Anatomy & Ordnance

* **Hulls & Armor:** Starships possess massive hull integrity tracks and heavy ablative armor plating that negates small-arms fire and shrugs off grazing void debris.
* **Ship Weapons:** Point-Defense Cannons (PDCs) for anti-missile interception, spinal-mount mass drivers, high-velocity coilguns, and high-yield torpedo arrays. See **[Ship Weapons](ships/Ship_Weapons.md)**.
* **Internal Modules:** Engineering bays, autoloader carousels, medical bays, hardened sensor matrices, and injector g-seats that reduce G-Strain. See **[Internal Modules](ships/Internal_Modules.md)**.
* **The Hangar Bay:** Complete technical profiles for standardized voidcraft, from nimble courier interceptors to heavy Flotilla battlecruisers. See **[The Hangar Bay](ships/Hangar_Bay.md)**.

Review core flight mechanics in **[Ship Rules](ships/Ship_Rules.md)** and track your vessel on the printable **[Crew & Starship Sheet](character/Crew_Sheet.html)**.

### Downtime & Operations

Between high-stakes orbital breaches and planetary drops, life aboard a Corsair vessel shifts into **Day-to-Day pacing**. Downtime provides players with structured agency to upgrade equipment, heal debilitating wounds, study new traits, and hustle for side income, while giving the GM a living economic clock.

#### The 24-Hour Downtime Cycle & Shift Model

A single Day-to-Day round represents **one full 24-hour day**. Each crew member receives **3 Day-to-Day Action Points (AP)** per day, corresponding to three 8-hour operational shifts:
* **Shift 1: Morning (08:00–16:00):** Primary station operations, shipyard maintenance, clinic visits, and market provisioning.
* **Shift 2: Afternoon (16:00–24:00):** Secondary projects, freelance contracts, simulator drills, and academic research.
* **Shift 3: Night (00:00–08:00):** Rest, medical recuperation, evening gigs, or system modifications.

#### Supplies & Operating Runway

A starship's operating runway is abstracted into **Supplies** (combining food rations, reactor coolant, life-support scrubber chemicals, and propellant gases).
* **Daily Consumption:** At the **End of the Round** (every 24 hours), the ship automatically consumes **1 Supply** from cargo. Paying this 1 Supply sustains life support and refreshes all crew members back to 3 Day-to-Day AP for the morning.
* **Zero Supply Deprivation:** If a ship enters a day with 0 Supplies, life support falters: auxiliary modules power down, starship repairs are blocked, crew members cannot gain XP, and every operative suffers **1 Light Wound and 1 Light Stress** each unsupplied morning.
* **Procuring Supplies:** Emergency rations cost $2,000 Credits per Supply, while bulk purchases offer major discounts ($20,000 Credits for an Industrial Haul of 20 Supplies). Cargo capacities limit reserves based on hull size (10 Supply for Small hulls up to 40+ for Large hulls).

#### Medical Care & The Ship Fund

* **Long Rest Recovery:** An operative resting in a bunk or medical bay makes an **Endurance + Willpower** Success Test during downtime, spending generated points to heal Light Wounds (1 SP per segment) or clear Aggravated Wounds (2 SP per segment). Station clinics and ship medbays provide additional recovery bonuses.
* **Starship Maintenance & The Ship Fund:** Starship hull repairs cost **200 Credits per hull segment**. To avoid bickering over personal pockets, crews maintain a collective **Ship Fund** escrow account fed by gross contract percentages to pay for docking berthing fees, fuel burns, and ordnance reloads.
* **Freelance Gigs:** Crew members can spend downtime shifts taking odd jobs from station bulletin boards. The master **d100 Freelance Gigs Table** provides a colorful variety of short jobs—from bouncer duty at a zero-g dive bar to recalibrating hydroponic pumps—earning quick credits and unique narrative rumors.

For full downtime procedures, trading bazaars, and freelance gigs, see **[Downtime & Operations](downtime/Downtime.md)** ([Downtime Reference Sheet](downtime/Downtime_Sheet.html) · [Freelance Gigs Sheet](downtime/Freelance_Gigs_Sheet.html)).

## Part V: Running the Game (The GM Section)

### Running the Game

Being a Game Master in *Corsair* is about more than adjudicating dice rolls; it is about operating a living, reactive world and challenging your players with tactical encounters, high-stakes investigations, and demanding moral dilemmas. Rather than treating time, social pressure, and adversaries as abstract narrative hand-waves, *Corsair* equips the Game Master with structured mechanical engines that transform every decision into a race against the clock.

For the primary GM manual, see **[Running the Game](gm/Running_the_Game.md)**.

#### Core Operational Guides

* **[Time Management & Pacing](gm/Time_Management.md):** Master the universal End-of-Round heartbeat, the 3x3 chip token model across all three pacing tiers (Moment to Moment, Place to Place, Day to Day), seamless zooming transitions, and tactical countdown tracks that keep player pressure palpable.
* **[Social Interactions & Negotiations](gm/Social_Interactions.md):** Turn tense dialogues, corporate council hearings, and underworld interrogations into tactical confrontations governed by Action Points, ticking Patience clocks, Determination Blocker Effects, and NPC counter-tactics.
* **[Stealth & Infiltration](gm/Stealth_and_Infiltration.md):** Adjudicate covert infiltrations, disguise craftsmanship, passive and active sensor networks, sensory downgrade penalties, and three-stage facility alarm states (Green: Routine Patrol, Yellow: Heightened Security, Red: Total Lockdown).
* **[Investigation & Exploration](gm/Investigation_and_Exploration.md):** Turn crime scenes, derelict starship hulls, and planetary reconnaissance into tactile, interactive environments governed by Keys (vital discoveries), Veils (soft observational obstacles), Shrouds (hard technological/mechanical locks), and the Lockdown Blocker engine.
* **[Chases & Pursuits](gm/Chases_and_Pursuits.md)** ([Reference Sheet](gm/Chases_and_Pursuits_Sheet.html)): Master high-speed kinetic evasions across foot, ground vehicle, and starship domains governed by Place-to-Place AP escape costs, strict disengagement guardrails, and severe interception penalties.
* **[Tracks](gm/Tracks.md):** The visual engine of the Sphere. Comprehensive rules for Progress, Countdown, Linked, Racing, and Tug-of-War Tracks used to visualize evolving crises, ticking reactor meltdowns, and contested tugs-of-war.

#### Adversaries & Encounter Design

* **[NPC Guidelines](gm/NPC_Guidelines.md):** Structured frameworks for building balanced, engaging enemies from disposable Tier 1 grunts to formidable Tier 4 bosses, utilizing streamlined physical/mental attribute baselines, property frameworks, and point budgets.
* **[Bestiary: Threats of the Sphere](gm/Bestiary.md):** A comprehensive catalog of ready-to-run stat blocks and tactical behaviors spanning station security forces, corporate black-ops strike teams, frontier outlaws, Scav raiding parties, and dangerous biological apex hazards.

#### Contracts & Mission Building

* **[Mission Generator](gm/Mission_Generator.md):** Rollable tables and procedural prompts to generate sanctioned corporate bounties, black-market salvage ops, and fringe contracts on the fly, complete with patrons, target locations, and unforeseen complications.
* **[Open Play Contracts](gm/Open_Play_Missions.md):** A suite of plug-and-play, bite-sized mission briefs featuring locations, targets, and credit rewards across Landfall, Nimbus, Pelagia, and Suncatcher.
