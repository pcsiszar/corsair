# Running The Game

When planning a session in Corsair, as the GM, you will have three options before you on how you want to run the game.
The first is a simple self-contained contract that lasts roughly the length of the session. The second is a campaign,
when you have a bigger story you want to tell over several sessions, maybe broken into chapters and interrupted by
different adventures to let things marinate for a bit. The third is free-play, which focuses on letting players take on
multiple bite-sized contracts that have the potential to bloom into something more interesting, focus on character
improvement, and let them explore the world in their own pace.

This section will guide you through how you'd want to run each of these different game modes, give you tools to help you
plan and execute, as well as some examples that are ready-baked for you to run.

## Running and Adventure

When starting out with Corsair, probably the best way to start is with an adventure that showcases whatever aspect of
the game that caught your attention in the first place, alongside any cool idea that you have. The most important thing 
in a good adventure is to find a good theme to focus on. A tried and true trope is always a nice place to start.

### Finding the Theme

Due to the nature of how Corsairs operate, nearly all adventures will be contracts the players will take on.

### Time is of the Essence

With the unique design of players always tracking their Action Points and the game tracking time via it's different
pacings, the GM is empowered to have the world act independently of the players at specific predetermined points in
time. Think of a dangerous criminal moving around the city when the players are supposed to track him down, or a patrol
moving along a predetermined route at specific intervals. This makes the game more interesting and more realistic,
but also, fair and predictable once your players understand the system by which you make the world move, and that
understanding is facilitated by the fact that they are also heavily involved in the same system with their Action
Points, Turns, and Rounds.

In order to properly wield this tool, you need to think about how the plot would unfold in your adventure if the players
did not interfere, and consequences would player actions have that would come into effect later down the line, not
immediately. An example of the former would be having a food-shortage escalate into riots after a certain period of time
if the players do not take action to stop it on the grand scale, or just simply how long it takes for a security patrol
to make the rounds on a small scale, so players know how much time they have to do what they intend to do, unseen. For
the latter, think how the mob-boss would likely try to escape a compound once they learn the players are making a
forceful entry at the far end, and would also probably call in grunts from the surrounding area to help them out.
Neither would happen immediately, more likely a few rounds later, determined by your discretion.

But keeping track of when is what supposed to happen is difficult, and you shouldn't have anything and everything
planned out. But including two or three scenarios where time is a major part of the equation will help you make an
experience more dramatic, meaningful, and unique.

### Moral Dillema

One of the differences between this game and other TTRPGs is that there is no clear prototypical adventure like a
heist, a dungeon crawl, or solving a mystery. As Corsairs, the players can be tasked with any of these, but it does not
get to the essence of what Corsairs are meant to be.

What makes Corsairs unique is that while they are not judge, jury, and executioner, they certainly have a thumb on the
scales. Sometimes this does not come into play as the problems they are sent to solve are morally black and white,
however, the true potential of the story is brought out when the players get to weigh in what is right and wrong using
their actions, and influence the final outcome.

So the name of the game then is moral dillema. Hunt down a dangerous fugitive only to find out along the chase that he
had good reasons and noble intentions and make the final call on whether to bring them in dead, alive, or not at all.
Get hired to kill a dangerous beast only to figure out that it's only dangerous because people started extracting
resources on its territory. Dismantle a plot to assassinate a corporate CEO and learn that the would-be assassins are
hired by the would-be successor who promises them a huge pile of credits to look the other way, just this once. Not
every mission has to include moral choice, but due to the nature of their job, Corsairs encounter these more often than
most, and they also have a certain level of authority and martial prowess to bend the rules and people the right way, or
what they think is the right way anyway.

If the dilemma is really difficult, players might even come to disagreements between themselves. And that is OK,
especially considering the whole premise of the Corsair Cell makeup is to have people of different backgrounds and
beliefs working together, but as the GM, you have a certain amount of responsibility to make sure that neither the topic
of the disagreements nor the disagreements themselves go to a place that makes your players feel uncomfortable.
