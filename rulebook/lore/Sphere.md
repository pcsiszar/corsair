# The Sphere: A Masterpiece of Celestial Engineering

The Sphere is an ancient, calculated machine—a solar system where the chaotic drift of space has been tamed into a
perfect, ticking clock. Centered around a stable Orange Star, this system hosts over 150 Earth-sized worlds maintained
in a delicate gravitational dance.

## The Hearth: The Primary

At the center sits a stable Orange Dwarf star. Steady and long-lived, its light is a permanent, amber-shifted "golden
hour" that provides a warm, consistent glow to every world in the system. Because it is cooler than a yellow sun, the
habitable rings are nestled much closer to its surface.

## The Three Rings

The habitable space is organized into three concentric rings. To account for the different circumferences of these
orbits, the density of planets increases as you move further from the star:

The Inner Ring (0.5 AU): The shortest track, containing 6 Gas Giants. A year here lasts roughly 150 days.

The Middle Ring (0.75 AU): The central track, containing 8 Gas Giants. A year here is a steady 240 days.

The Outer Ring (1.1 AU): The longest track, containing 12 Gas Giants. A year here stretches to over 400 days.

## The Resonance Chain

To prevent these worlds from colliding, the rings are locked into a Laplace Resonance. This is a mathematical "chain"
where the orbital periods are exact ratios (4:2:1). For every one orbit the Outer Ring makes, the Mid Ring makes two,
and the Inner Ring makes four. This ensures their gravitational pulls are rhythmic "nudges" that keep every world in
its proper place.

## The Anchors: Gas Giants

Within these three resonant rings sit 26 Gas Giants. These massive bodies are the gravitational hubs of the Sphere.
They act as "Parent Planets," providing the magnetic shields that protect their moons from solar radiation and
keeping the smaller worlds from drifting out of their assigned rings.

## The Binary Pairs: The Sister Worlds

Most of the habitable real estate exists as Binary Pairs. These are two Earth-sized planets orbiting a shared center
of gravity, which in turn orbits a Gas Giant. Because the two worlds are only 100,000 km apart, they have stopped
spinning independently and "stare" at each other eternally. From one side of your world, the Sister Planet hangs
motionless in the sky—a massive marble that never rises or sets. Once every 30-hour "day," the Sister Planet passes
directly in front of the sun. This causes a total solar eclipse known as the Daily Shadow, a brief hour of cool
twilight. Gravity from the twin is constant, so the oceans do not sweep across the globe. Instead, they form a
Permanent Tidal Bulge toward the Sister Planet, making one hemisphere deeply flooded and the other shallow.

## The Trojans

Floating 60 degrees ahead and behind each Gas Giant are the Trojan Worlds. These planets sit in Lagrange
Points—gravitational "pockets" where the pull of the Sun and the Gas Giant perfectly cancel each other out. These are
often lone planets with no "Sister," giving them an unobstructed view of the deep stars.

## The Watchers

Beyond the flat plane of the rings, a few rare bodies exist in Polar Orbits. These worlds dive "over the top" of the
star, traveling vertically relative to the rest of the system. From the top of their orbit, the entire Sphere is
visible below as a flat, glowing disc—a literal map of the 156-world machine.
